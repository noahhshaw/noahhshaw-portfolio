# Naps by Age — Wake Windows and Architecture

## Why naps matter operationally

Naps determine when bedtime can land, how rested the parent is, and whether the baby is overtired by evening. Overtiredness is the single biggest amplifier of bedtime resistance and night wakings — drive the nap quality and quantity, and night sleep usually follows.

## Wake windows by age

The "wake window" is the time between the end of one sleep period and the start of the next. Babies have shorter wake windows than adults; pushing through them produces overtiredness, not "tiring her out."

| Age | Wake window | Naps/day | Total day sleep | Total 24h sleep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–6 wk | 45–60 min | 4–6 (irregular) | 6–8h | 14–17h |
| 6–12 wk | 60–90 min | 4–5 | 5–6h | 14–16h |
| 3–4 mo | 75–120 min | 3–4 | 4–5h | 14–15h |
| 4–6 mo | 2–2.5h | 3 | 3–4h | 13–15h |
| 6–9 mo | 2.5–3h | 2 (transition from 3) | 2.5–3.5h | 13–14h |
| 9–12 mo | 3–4h | 2 | 2–3h | 12–14h |
| 12–18 mo | 4–5h | 1–2 (transition to 1) | 1.5–3h | 11–14h |

These are population averages; individual babies vary by ±20–30 min routinely.

## Nap transitions

- **5→4 naps** around 12 weeks.
- **4→3 naps** around 4 months (often coinciding with 4-month regression).
- **3→2 naps** around 7–9 months. The third nap fades first; babies often resist the late-afternoon nap once awake-stretches lengthen.
- **2→1 naps** around 14–18 months. Crosses into year 2.

Each transition takes 2–4 weeks and typically produces sleep disruption while the new schedule consolidates.

## The "schedule" debate

Two camps:

### Schedule-driven (e.g., Babywise, Moms on Call)
Set nap times by clock; wake the baby if she sleeps too long. Predictable; produces stable rhythm; some babies resist the imposed timing and cry through naps.

### Wake-window-driven (Taking Cara Babies, Precious Little Sleep)
Use elapsed wake time as the cue; let baby sleep until she wakes naturally. More flexible; harder to plan around (no fixed schedule); usually produces better-quality naps for sensitive babies.

Empirical evidence on which produces better total sleep is mixed. Most pediatric sleep specialists recommend a **wake-window-driven approach in months 0–6**, transitioning to a **mostly-clock approach by 9–12 months** as the baby's circadian system consolidates.

## Operational rules of thumb

- **First nap of the day is usually the easiest** to install; protect it.
- **Last nap of the day should end by 16:30–17:00** to allow bedtime around 18:30–19:30.
- **30–45 minute "catnaps"** are typical until 4 mo; consolidating into longer naps tracks the same biological transition as the 4-month sleep architecture change.
- **Crib-only naps** support nighttime crib associations. Stroller and contact naps are operational tools but should not be the only place baby sleeps if you're working toward consolidated night sleep.

## Common questions

**"She's only napping 30 minutes — what's wrong?"** Likely nothing. 30–45 min naps are common 2–4 mo, age out around 5–6 mo for many infants. If she wakes happy and the next wake window is intact, the catnap is working.

**"She skipped a nap today — should I bring bedtime forward?"** Yes. Lost daytime sleep should be banked at the next sleep period; usually that means an earlier bedtime by 30–60 min, not a "make-up" 5pm nap.

**"Can I let her sleep more than [X] hours during the day?"** Yes, with one caveat: if total day sleep is so long that night sleep is fragmenting (waking at 4am ready to go), consider capping the last nap.

## Watch-fors

- `[low concern]` Variable nap quality across days; transitions taking 2–4 weeks.
- `[monitor]` Total 24h sleep persistently below table range by >20%.
- `[call within 24h]` Sudden persistent sleep refusal accompanying illness signs.
- `[call now]` Persistent inability to wake baby for feeds.
