# Screen Time

## What the AAP actually says

AAP 2016 policy (still current as of 2026)[1]:
- **Under 18 months**: avoid all digital media use except video chatting with family.
- **18–24 months**: if introducing media, choose high-quality programming, watch with the child, and avoid solo use.
- **2–5 years**: limit to 1 hour/day of high-quality programming, co-viewed.
- **6+ years**: consistent limits, no displacement of sleep/physical activity/in-person interaction.

The video-chat exception is principled: video chat with a familiar adult is interactive and resembles live social interaction, which is the kind of input infant brains use for language and social learning. Passive media — even "educational" — is not.

## What the evidence shows

### Background TV is bad

Background television (TV on while baby is in the room, even if she's not watching) is associated with:
- Reduced parent-child verbal interaction.
- Reduced quality of parent-child play.
- Slower expressive vocabulary growth.

Evidence: multiple observational and quasi-experimental studies. Effect sizes small but consistent. Christakis et al., "The effects of infant media usage" (Arch Pediatr Adolesc Med 2009)[2].

### Foreground "educational" video does not deliver claimed benefits

Zimmerman et al. (2007) showed *Baby Einstein*-type video exposure under 24 mo correlated with *slower* vocabulary acquisition, not faster[3]. The "video deficit" — infants don't transfer learning from video as they do from live interaction — is well-replicated.

### Video chat is fine

DeLoache and colleagues showed that 6-mo-olds responded socially to live video chat (vs. recorded video) in ways consistent with face-to-face interaction. The interactivity is the key feature.

## Practical guidance

### Under 18 months — recommended approach

- **No background TV**, full stop. If you watch TV, do it after bedtime.
- **Video chat OK** with grandparents, traveling parent, etc. Limit to 10–15 min sessions; participate alongside baby.
- **No phones in stroller/car seat for entertainment.** This is a hard line — once normalized, it's hard to undo.
- **No "while you're cooking/working" tablet.** You cannot work or cook while caring for an infant; either get childcare or time-shift the task.
- **Phone use by parents**: avoid phone-while-feeding when possible. Phone use during interaction directly displaces conversational turns.

### 18–24 months — soft transition

- High-quality programming (Sesame Street, Daniel Tiger) for occasional co-viewing (15–30 min).
- Always watched with parent; conversation about content during/after.
- No solo viewing in this window.

### Long-term frame

The kids who do best academically and socially in adolescence had limited screen exposure in early childhood. The mechanism is plausibly displacement: every hour of screens displaces an hour of face-to-face interaction, book reading, outdoor play, music, free play. That displacement is the issue, not the content per se.

## What to do instead

- **Play independently in a baby-safe space** while parent is nearby (allows hands-free time).
- **Babywearing** for parent productivity if the baby is content.
- **Video chat with grandparents**: counts as social interaction, not screen time.
- **Audio for the parent**: podcast/audiobook in the parent's earbud while caring for baby. Doesn't reach baby's auditory environment as background noise to the same degree as TV.

## Watch-fors

- `[low concern]` Occasional video chat 5–10 min; phone glance while soothing.
- `[monitor]` Routine background TV; phone-during-feed becoming habitual.
- `[call within 24h]` (No screen-related call-within-24h scenarios.)

## Special-circumstance notes

- **Plane travel and other extreme contexts**: AAP's recommendation acknowledges real-world constraints. A 30-min video on a 6-hour flight is not catastrophic. The issue is daily use.
- **Sick days**: same — episodic exposure during illness is not pattern-forming.
- **Family movies once kids are 2+**: family co-viewing is a different category than infant solo screen.
