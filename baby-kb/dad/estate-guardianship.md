# Estate Planning and Guardianship

## What you must have in place

For new parents with minor children, four documents:

1. **Last Will and Testament** (or pour-over will if using a trust).
2. **Revocable Living Trust** (recommended for most families above ~$500K net worth or owning real estate).
3. **Guardianship designation** for minor children (typically embedded in will or trust).
4. **Healthcare power of attorney + living will** for each adult.

Plus: financial power of attorney, beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance, asset titling alignment.

Without these, on premature death the state's intestacy rules apply; the probate court appoints a guardian; assets are frozen during probate (months to years); life insurance proceeds get distributed by court-supervised guardian-ad-litem to the child at age 18.

## Will vs Trust — which to use

### Will-only

- Simpler, cheaper to draft ($300–800 with attorney; $100–300 for online services like LegalZoom — caveat lector).
- **All assets pass through probate** — public, slow (6–18 months typical), $5K–$20K in court costs.
- **Guardianship still designated through will**.
- Reasonable for: families with simple finances, low real-estate exposure, no significant assets.

### Will + Revocable Living Trust

- More complex, more expensive to draft ($1,500–4,500 with attorney).
- **Assets in the trust avoid probate** — private, fast, low cost at death.
- **Guardianship designated separately** in pour-over will.
- **Supports staged distribution** — children don't get lump sum at 18; can stage at e.g., 25% at 25, 50% at 30, remainder at 35.
- Recommended for: families with real estate, total assets >$500K-$1M, multiple state residences, minor children with substantial inheritance.

For Bay Area / NYC families with home + investment assets + life insurance trust assets, a revocable living trust with pour-over will is the right answer.

## The trust structure

Typical structure:

1. **Revocable Living Trust** — both spouses as grantors and trustees; primary trust holds non-retirement assets.
2. **Pour-over will** — anything not in the trust at death gets added to the trust via probate.
3. **Bypass / credit-shelter trust** — for estate-tax planning above the federal exemption (currently $13.99M per person 2026, sunsetting ~$7M/person in 2026 absent legislation).
4. **Insurance trust (ILIT)** — life insurance owned by an irrevocable trust to keep proceeds out of taxable estate. Useful at high net worth.
5. **Special needs trust** — only if a beneficiary may need it.

Most first-time-baby families need (1) and (2). (3)–(5) are wealth-tier-specific and require an estate attorney.

## Guardianship designation

The most important single decision in your estate documents.

### Whom to name

- **Primary guardian**: the person who will raise the child.
- **Contingent guardian**: backup if primary cannot serve.
- **Trustee for finances** (separate from guardian): the person who manages money for the child's benefit.

**Why split guardian and trustee**: the best at raising kids isn't necessarily the best with money; separating power forces collaboration and reduces conflicts of interest.

### Considerations

- **Geographic stability**: guardian's home state, school district, community.
- **Relationship with child**: existing bond; will be the lived environment.
- **Values alignment**: religious, educational, lifestyle.
- **Age/health of guardian**: ideally not significantly older than you (won't outlive child to 18).
- **Their consent**: discuss with the named guardian before naming them; revisit annually.
- **Backups**: name 2–3 levels of contingents.

### Discuss explicitly

This is the conversation many couples avoid. Make it concrete:
- "If we both die, we want X to raise her."
- X needs to know they're named. Ask.
- Document why — guardianship is reviewable in court if challenged; written rationale strengthens enforceability.

## Healthcare and financial powers of attorney

For each spouse:

- **Healthcare POA / Living Will**: medical decisions if incapacitated. Do not need separate documents in most states — one combined form.
- **Financial POA**: who manages finances if incapacitated. Often the spouse, with a contingent.
- **HIPAA authorization**: each spouse authorizes the other to access health info.

Without these, if one spouse is incapacitated mid-pregnancy or postpartum, the other may not be able to make decisions or access information without court intervention.

## Beneficiary alignment

Common error: estate plan + trust set up correctly, but 401(k) and life insurance beneficiaries still listed as "ex-girlfriend" or "parent" from before marriage. **Beneficiary designations supersede the will.**

Audit:
- 401(k), 403(b), IRA, Roth IRA — beneficiary should be spouse primary, trust contingent.
- Life insurance — same.
- Bank accounts (TOD/POD designations).
- Brokerage accounts (TOD).

## Cost

Full estate package with attorney: $2,500–6,000 in major metros. Worth the cost — DIY estate planning is the worst place to economize.

**Recommended attorneys** are fee-only or flat-fee. Avoid attorneys whose fee structure incentivizes selling specific products (annuities, whole life).

## Annual review

Revisit annually:
- Major life events (additional children, move, divorce, death of guardian).
- Asset value changes.
- Tax law changes (federal exemption, state rules).
- Guardian still willing/appropriate.

## What to do this week (if you don't have docs)

1. **Identify a trust-and-estate attorney** (referral from financial advisor, friend, or local bar association).
2. **Initial meeting** (30–60 min, often free).
3. **Draft documents** — typically 4–8 weeks.
4. **Sign and notarize**.
5. **Fund the trust** — retitle assets to the trust (deed for real estate, account ownership change for brokerages).
6. **Update beneficiary designations**.
7. **Tell named guardian and trustee** they're named.
