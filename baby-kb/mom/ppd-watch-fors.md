# Postpartum Depression and Anxiety — Watch-fors

## What's normal vs not

### Baby blues (50–80% of new mothers)

- **Onset**: days 2–5 postpartum.
- **Peak**: days 4–6.
- **Resolution**: by day 14.
- **Symptoms**: tearfulness, mood swings, anxiety, sleep disruption beyond what baby imposes, feeling overwhelmed.
- **Mechanism**: hormonal shift (estrogen/progesterone drop dramatically post-delivery).
- **What to do**: monitor; ensure rest, food, support. Self-resolves.

### Postpartum depression (10–20% of new mothers)

- **Onset**: weeks 2–8 most common; can present up to 12 months postpartum.
- **Symptoms**: persistent low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness, excessive guilt, sleep disruption disproportionate, appetite changes, difficulty bonding, intrusive thoughts.
- **Severity**: ranges from mild (functional but suffering) to severe (suicidal ideation, inability to care for self/baby).
- **Treatment**: therapy (CBT, IPT), medication (SSRIs are first-line; sertraline and others compatible with breastfeeding), or both.

### Postpartum anxiety (15% of new mothers)

- **Symptoms**: racing thoughts, panic attacks, hypervigilance about baby, intrusive worry, physical symptoms (chest tightness, GI upset).
- **Often co-occurs with PPD**.
- **Treatment**: same as PPD.

### Postpartum OCD (3–5% of new mothers)

- **Symptoms**: intrusive thoughts (often about harm coming to baby — typically EGO-DYSTONIC, meaning the mother is horrified by them); compulsive behaviors (checking on baby repeatedly, avoiding being alone with baby).
- **Important**: ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts ("what if I drop her down the stairs?") are NOT a sign of intent to harm; they are a sign of OCD/PPA. Many mothers don't disclose because they fear being seen as dangerous. Treatable, common.
- **Distinction from psychosis**: psychotic thoughts are believed; intrusive thoughts are unwanted and distressing.

### Postpartum psychosis (1–2 in 1,000 births — emergent)

- **Onset**: typically first 2 weeks, can be later.
- **Symptoms**: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking, dramatic mood swings, sleep complete loss, paranoia. Can include thoughts that the baby is not real, is possessed, etc.
- **This is a psychiatric emergency.** Risk to mother and baby is real.
- **Action**: ER, call 911 if acute; do not leave mother alone with baby.

## Screening — EPDS

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale is the standard screening tool[1].

- **10 items**, self-report, scored 0–3 each, total 0–30.
- **Score >10**: possible depression — further evaluation recommended.
- **Score ≥13**: likely depression.
- **Item 10 (self-harm thoughts)**: any score >0 is significant regardless of total.

ACOG recommends EPDS or equivalent screen at least once during pregnancy and at the 6-week postpartum visit[2]. Many practices screen at every prenatal/postpartum visit.

The EPDS is freely available; you can self-administer between scheduled visits. Online calculators (Perinatology.com, MDCalc) score it.

## What to actually do

### If you score 5–10 on EPDS

- Increase support: more sleep, more help with baby, more outings.
- Talk to OB and pediatrician at the next visit.
- Consider a "monitor weekly" check-in with self-reporting.

### If you score 10–13

- Call OB or PCP within 24h for evaluation.
- Consider therapy referral. Postpartum-specialized therapists (Postpartum Support International maintains a directory) are most-effective.
- Watch carefully; can escalate.

### If you score ≥13 OR have any score on item 10 (self-harm)

- Same-day call to OB or psychiatric care.
- Postpartum Support International helpline: 1-800-944-4773.
- 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline if acute.

### If you suspect psychosis (delusions, severe disorientation, suicidal/infanticidal ideation)

- ER same-day. Do not leave mother alone with baby.
- 911 if acute.

## Treatment — what's safe in breastfeeding

The evidence base is robust enough that medication treatment of PPD does not require weaning in most cases.

- **Sertraline (Zoloft)** — most-prescribed SSRI in lactation; minimal infant exposure via milk. First-line.
- **Escitalopram (Lexapro)** — also commonly used.
- **Fluoxetine (Prozac)** — longer half-life; pediatricians sometimes prefer alternatives but acceptable.
- **Paroxetine (Paxil)** — historically common; some evidence of higher infant exposure; less first-line now.
- **Bupropion (Wellbutrin)** — alternative for those who don't tolerate SSRIs; potentially affects supply.
- **Brexanolone (Zulresso) / Zuranolone (Zurzuvae)** — newer FDA-approved postpartum-specific antidepressants; faster onset; cost and access vary.
- **Therapy alone**: CBT, IPT have evidence equivalent to medication for mild-moderate PPD.

A psychiatrist (preferably reproductive psychiatry sub-specialist; see Massachusetts General Hospital Center for Women's Mental Health, https://womensmentalhealth.org) is the ideal prescriber.

## Risk factors

- **Personal history of depression** or anxiety (highest risk factor).
- **Family history** of perinatal mood disorders.
- **Difficult pregnancy or delivery**.
- **NICU stay** for baby.
- **Limited social support**.
- **Sleep deprivation severe**.
- **Major life stress** (move, job change, financial stress, relationship strain).
- **Hormonal sensitivity** (PMDD history).

## Father's role

The most-protective single factor (per multiple studies) is partner support. Concrete actions:

- **Attend OB visits** through pregnancy and the 6-week postpartum.
- **Ask explicitly** at 1, 2, 4, 6 weeks: "How are you doing today, on a scale of 1–10? What's the lowest moment of the day? Anything I can take off your plate?"
- **Watch for symptoms**, gently. Not "are you depressed" — "you seem more tired/withdrawn/sad than usual; would it help to talk to the OB?"
- **Co-pilot on care**: equal share of nighttime, equal share of childcare logistics. The "default-parent burden" is a major PPD driver.
- **If she's not improving**: schedule the call. Don't wait for her to do it.

## When to call vs go to ER

- `[monitor]` Brief tearfulness in first 2 weeks (baby blues).
- `[call within 24h]` EPDS >10; persistent low mood beyond 2 weeks postpartum; intrusive thoughts; significant anxiety affecting function.
- `[call now]` Suicidal ideation; thoughts of harming baby; psychotic symptoms; complete loss of sleep over multiple days; severe self-care deterioration.
